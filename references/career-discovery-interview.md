# Career Discovery Interview

Use this reference to conduct a source-grounded interview that reveals the strongest defensible version of the user's career and helps determine what work they should pursue next.

## Start from the record

Review the available resume, LinkedIn profile, performance reviews, project notes, interview preparation, and other supplied materials before asking questions.

Open the interview with a concise provisional readout:

- what the documents clearly establish
- the likely level and scope demonstrated
- the strongest recurring themes
- apparent gaps, contradictions, or undersold work
- the questions most likely to change the positioning or target roles

Label facts, inferences, and open questions distinctly. Invite the user to correct the initial interpretation.

## Interview adaptively

Ask a small group of related questions at a time so the conversation feels like an interview rather than a form. Prefer specific, answerable questions over prompts such as "tell me more about your background."

Follow promising evidence. If an answer reveals substantial ownership, impact, or a new career direction, explore it before moving to the next topic. Do not ask questions already answered by the source material unless validation is genuinely needed.

Useful areas of inquiry include:

### Career chronology and transitions

- Why did the user enter, leave, or change each meaningful role?
- Which transitions represented an increase or decrease in actual scope?
- Are short tenures, title changes, overlapping work, or employment gaps likely to need context?

Do not assume that a nonlinear path is a weakness.

### Actual operating scope

- What was the user accountable for, regardless of title?
- Which decisions could they make directly?
- What required influence, sponsorship, or negotiation?
- What teams, customers, revenue, budgets, systems, regions, or programs were affected?
- How much ambiguity, organizational complexity, or executive exposure was involved?

### Evidence and outcomes

For important accomplishments, establish:

1. What was true before the work began?
2. What was the user's mandate?
3. What did the user personally decide, create, change, or lead?
4. What made the work difficult?
5. What operating mechanisms, standards, systems, or relationships did they build?
6. What changed as a result?
7. What metric, artifact, feedback, or external signal supports that result?
8. What can safely be shared on a resume, on LinkedIn, or only in an interview?

Separate individual contribution from team outcomes without minimizing legitimate leadership.

### Strengths and working style

- What kinds of difficult problems repeatedly find their way to the user?
- What do leaders, peers, reports, or customers consistently rely on them for?
- Which accomplishments best represent how they operate?
- What positive themes recur across performance reviews or feedback?
- Where does the user have unusual combinations of domain, functional, technical, or leadership fluency?

### Direction, energy, and anti-goals

- Which work gives the user energy, and which work drains it?
- What do they want to spend more or less time doing?
- Do they want people leadership, individual ownership, or a combination?
- What kinds of problems, environments, leaders, customers, or company stages bring out their best work?
- Which past responsibilities are they capable of doing but do not want as the center of the next role?
- What would make a role look attractive initially but become a poor move in practice?

Treat preferences as strategy inputs, not as evidence of capability.

### The intended move

Explore desired level, scope, compensation, work model, geography, travel, risk, industries, company stage, and acceptable tradeoffs. Test more than one plausible future when the record supports multiple directions.

Do not force the user to name a target title before the evidence and preferences have been synthesized. Offer hypotheses such as:

- a high-confidence continuation of demonstrated work
- a credible upward stretch
- an adjacent direction using the same underlying strengths

Explain the evidence, advantages, and risks behind each hypothesis.

## Drill down without interrogating

When an answer is vague, ask for the missing dimension that matters most: ownership, scale, complexity, action, result, or proof. Do not repeatedly demand exact metrics when none exist. Look for credible alternatives such as adoption, cycle time, reach, executive sponsorship, customer feedback, risk reduction, organizational reuse, or scope entrusted.

Challenge inconsistencies respectfully. A useful pattern is:

> The documents suggest [interpretation], while your answer suggests [different interpretation]. Which is closer to the actual scope?

Allow the user to skip sensitive questions. Do not pressure them to disclose confidential employer information, compensation, health information, protected characteristics, or personal circumstances.

## Synthesize as the interview progresses

After each meaningful round:

1. summarize what changed in the working understanding
2. identify the strongest new evidence
3. note remaining uncertainty
4. let the user correct the record

Stop when additional questioning is unlikely to materially change the search strategy, positioning, or evidence base. The goal is useful depth, not exhaustive biography.

## Turn discovery into action

At the end, provide:

- the strongest factual career profile
- highest demonstrated operating scope
- a short list of signature proof points
- recommended resume changes and missing evidence to recover
- recommended LinkedIn headline, About narrative, experience emphasis, and skills or search language
- primary, stretch, adjacent, and avoid role lanes
- work themes and environments to prioritize or avoid
- initial natural-language job searches
- a small set of unresolved questions, if any
- the next three to five actions

Keep public-facing recommendations safe for public use. Reserve confidential metrics, private feedback, and compensation for strategy or interview preparation as appropriate.
